Weekend Fade: fading weekend drift in 24/7 stock perps

Hyperliquid's US stock perpetuals trade through the weekend while the stock market is shut. Single stocks drift away from the market on thin trading, and on Sunday at 20:05 ET, when real prices return, about — of that drift comes back. We fade it, hedged against the Nasdaq-100 perp. The effect is strong and statistically clear; how much of it you keep depends almost entirely on how well you are filled.

High level

The idea in one minute

You can trade "copies" of US stocks such as Nvidia or Rocket Lab on Hyperliquid at any hour, including weekends. On weekends there is no real stock price to follow, so each perp's price is set only by whoever happens to trade it. A handful of trades can push a stock 2% above or below where the rest of the market sits.

On Sunday evening the real world comes back. At 18:00 ET index futures reopen, and at 20:05 ET each stock perp's reference price switches back to real overnight trading (Blue Ocean ATS). Prices that drifted for no reason get pulled back.

The agent measures each stock's weekend move relative to the market, bets that large drifts partly reverse, and hedges with the Nasdaq-100 perp so that only the stock-specific drift matters. It enters at 18:00 Sunday and exits at 04:00 Monday.

The weekend as the perps see it. Grey band: no real price for stocks, so each perp follows a 30-minute average of its own order book. The trade lives in the coloured span.
Fundamentals and market structure

Why the drift should exist, and why it should reverse

Who trades a stock perp on a Saturday. Crypto-native traders, retail accounts reacting to headlines and social media, and a few market makers. Professional stock investors are absent because their market is shut. Volume is thin: in the Sunday 18:00 hour the median trade's stock traded about $259k, the median smaller name only $178k, and a quarter of all trades were in stocks doing under $49k. A weekday hour is often 10 to 100 times that.

No anchor and no hedge. From Friday 20:00 to Sunday 20:05 ET, trade[XYZ] prices each stock perp from an exponential average of its own order book (30-minute time constant), starting from Friday's last real price. Market makers cannot hedge in the real stock, so they quote wider and lean against order flow. One-sided flow therefore moves the price further than it would on a weekday.

Macro news is real; single-stock drift mostly isn't. When something happens over a weekend (a war headline, a tariff, a crypto crash), it moves the whole market, and the Nasdaq-100 perp prices it. We subtract that. What is left is the stock-specific part, which over a weekend is usually flow and sentiment rather than news, because companies rarely publish on Saturdays.

The correction has a fixed time. At 18:00 ET Sunday CME index futures reopen and the index perp starts following them. At 20:05 ET stock perps switch to Blue Ocean overnight prices, with each oracle update limited to ±0.5%, so a large gap closes over several minutes rather than instantly. Funding then pulls the perp toward that real price.

Why weeknights should not work. Monday to Thursday the perps follow real after-hours and overnight prices almost continuously. A stock that moves at night has usually moved for a reason (earnings, guidance), and real news does not reverse. That gives a built-in placebo test.

What we set out to test

Hypothesis and the predictions that could falsify it

H1: a stock perp's weekend drift relative to the index partly reverses after Sunday 18:00 ET. H0: the drift is information; what happens next is unrelated to it. If H1 is right, all of these must hold: (a) weekends: next-period excess return is negatively related to the weekend gap (slope b < 0) (b) weeknights, same rule: no relation (b ≈ 0) (c) most of the reversal happens after the 20:05 ET switch to real prices (d) betting on continuation (momentum) loses money (e) the effect is not one stock, one sector or one weekend

Every one of (a)–(e) is checked below. Any one failing would have killed the idea.

The maths

Prices: P_s(t) stock perp, P_I(t) Nasdaq-100 perp (xyz:XYZ100). All returns are log returns in basis points: r(a→b) = 10,000 × ln( P(b) / P(a) ) 1. Weekend gap (the signal), Friday 16:00 → Sunday 17:00 ET: g = r_s(Fri16→Sun17) − r_I(Fri16→Sun17) 2. Rule. Trade only if |g| ≥ k, with k = 50bp for big names and 150bp otherwise, and |g| < 800bp: side = −sign(g) (stock ran ahead → short it; stock lagged → buy it) 3. Hedged position: $N of stock on `side`, and $N of the index on the opposite side. 4. Outcome, entry Sunday 18:00 → exit Monday 04:00: y = r_s(Sun18→Mon04) − r_I(Sun18→Mon04) gross P&L (bp of N) = side × y 5. Costs (bp of N), four fills: stock: 2 × fee + 2 × half-spread fee 0.9bp (growth mode) or 9bp; half-spread 1bp big, 5bp other index: 2 × (0.9bp fee + 0.5bp slippage) net = side × y − costs $ P&L = N × net / 10,000 6. Size: N = min( $50k big / $10k other , 0.10 × last-hour $ volume ) 7. Does the gap come back? Regress the outcome on the gap across all stock-weekends: y_{i,w} = a + b × g_{i,w} + e_{i,w} b = −0.40 means 40% of the gap reverses. Standard errors are clustered two ways (by weekend and by stock), because stocks in one weekend share news and one stock's weekends share its traits. 8. Strategy statistics use one number per weekend (the average net of that weekend's trades): t = mean / (sd / √W) annualised Sharpe = mean / sd × √52 Newey–West t allows for autocorrelation between weekends. Sign-flip test: flip each weekend's sign at random 20,000 times; p = share of shuffles with |mean| ≥ the real one. Bootstrap: resample weekends with replacement 20,000 times; 2.5th–97.5th percentile of the mean.

Choosing the version

Which version is best

We varied the four free choices: the trigger for big names (25/50/100bp), for other stocks (100/150/200/300bp), the entry hour (18:00/19:00/20:00) and the exit (Sun 21:00, Mon 00:00, Mon 04:00, Mon 09:00): — versions, all with fair fills. The real caveat

Fills and capacity

Hyperliquid does not publish historical order books, so we cannot know the exact prices these trades would have received. We bracket them with three assumptions. The difference between them is larger than the edge itself, so this is the number to watch in the forward test.

Big names vs other stocks (fair fills)

Big names trigger less often and earn less per trade, but their order books are deep enough that "fair fills" is realistic. Small names earn more on paper but are the ones most exposed to bad fills.

Baselines and placebo (fair fills)

The benchmark is doing nothing (0%). Following the gap instead of fading it loses heavily, and the same rule on weeknights earns nothing.
